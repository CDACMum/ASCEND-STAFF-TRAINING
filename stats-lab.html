Chapter 1

Centre and outliers

What does "typical" mean, and which single number can one bad value ruin?

Expert view

A summary statistic is a decision about what to ignore. The mean uses every value, so it inherits every outlier: one observation can drag it anywhere, which is why statisticians say its breakdown point is zero. The median uses only the order of the values and survives until half the data is contaminated.

The IQR fence rule flags a value when it lies more than 1.5 × IQR beyond the quartiles. It judges a point against the middle half of the data, using quartiles that outliers barely move, so the outlier cannot hide itself by inflating the yardstick.

Common trap

Quoting a mean for salaries, delivery times or claim sizes without looking at the shape. When the mean sits well above the median the column is right-skewed: report the median and the IQR, and say so.

Chapter 2

Standard deviation, in seven scrolls

Scroll. Each step adds one idea to the picture on the right. You can drag the dots at any time.

Step 1

Start with measurements

Eight values on a line. The only question: how spread out are they? Range uses two of the eight values. We want a number that uses all of them.

Step 2

Find the balance point

The mean is where the line would balance if every dot had equal weight. It is the natural reference: spread means distance from here.

Step 3

Measure each deviation

Each arrow is one deviation, value minus mean. Left of the mean it is negative, right of it positive. Add them up and you always get exactly zero, which is the definition of a balance point, so we cannot average them as they stand.

Step 4

Square each deviation

Squaring removes the sign, and it is geometric: a deviation of 4 becomes a square sixteen times the area of a deviation of 1. Far-away points get a loud voice. This is a choice, and the reason standard deviation reacts strongly to outliers.

Step 5

Add the areas

The sum of squared deviations (SSD) is the total area of all the squares. It grows with the number of points as well as with the spread, so it cannot be compared across datasets yet.

Step 6

Average the areas: variance

Divide the total area by n (population) or n − 1 (sample). The result is the area of the "average square". The unit is the square of your data unit, minutes squared or rupees squared, which nobody can picture.

Step 7

Take the square root: standard deviation

The side of the average square is the standard deviation, back in the original unit. Read it as "a typical distance from the mean". For bell-shaped data, about 68% of values fall within one SD of the mean and 95% within two.

Expert view

The standard deviation is the root-mean-square distance from the mean. Because the sample mean is computed from the same data, deviations from it are systematically smaller than deviations from the true, unknown mean. Dividing by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) removes that bias in the variance. The sample SD itself is still slightly biased low, which is acceptable.

Why n − 1? Watch it happen

population: mean 50, SD 10, true variance 100

Each sample's variance is computed twice: dividing by n (orange) and by n − 1 (violet). Averaged over many samples, only n − 1 lands on 100. The dividing-by-n estimate settles at 100 × (n − 1)/n, and the gap is largest for small samples.

Common trap

Using np.var(x) or np.std(x) on a sample and assuming it is the sample statistic. NumPy divides by n unless you pass ddof=1; pandas' .std() divides by n − 1 by default. Know which one you called.

Chapter 3

Shape: skewness and kurtosis

Two columns can share a mean and an SD and still behave very differently.

Expert view

Skewness is the third standardised moment: it measures asymmetry. A positive value means a long right tail (salaries, claim sizes), and the mean is pulled above the median. A negative value means a long left tail. The name follows the tail, not the peak.

Excess kurtosis (the scipy and pandas convention) is the fourth standardised moment minus 3, so the normal curve scores 0. Positive means heavier tails and a sharper peak than normal: more surprises. Negative means lighter tails. For risk, kurtosis is the number that says how often extremes occur.

Chapter 4

The normal curve and z-scores

Bangalore to Mumbai: mean 15 hours, SD 2 hours. How likely is a drive under 14?

Expert view

A normal curve is fully described by two numbers. A z-score, z = (x − μ) / σ, counts how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean, which turns every normal problem into one standard problem. cdf answers "how much probability lies to the left of x"; ppf is its inverse, "which x has this much to its left".

Everything you meet later (confidence intervals, p-values, control limits) is a probability under this curve or a close relative of it.

Chapter 5

Why averages behave: the central limit theorem

Start from a lopsided population. Average samples of it. Watch a bell appear.

Expert view

If you repeatedly draw samples of size n from any population with a finite variance, the distribution of the sample means approaches a normal curve centred on the population mean, with spread σ/√n (the standard error). The population does not have to be normal; n only has to be large enough, and heavier skew needs a larger n.

This is the licence behind z-tests, t-tests and confidence intervals on averages. It also explains the "n ≥ 30" rule of thumb taught in class: not magic, just usually enough for moderate skew.

Chapter 6

Distributions you can query

Binomial, Poisson, exponential and Student's t, with the scipy call written for you.

Expert view

Choose the distribution from the story, not the data. Binomial: a fixed number of independent yes/no trials with known p. Poisson: events counted in a window when you know the average rate. Exponential: the waiting time between those Poisson events. t: a bell with heavier tails, used for means when σ is estimated from a small sample.

Then translate the English: exactly k is pmf; at most k is cdf; at least k is sf(k − 1) for counts; between a and b inclusive is cdf(b) − cdf(a − 1).

Common trap

expon.cdf(7, 365/70) does not use 365/70 as the mean. After x, scipy's next positional argument is loc, a shift. The mean goes in scale: expon.cdf(7, scale=365/70) gives 0.739; the shifted version gives 0.832. Try it in the explorer above (Exponential tab, mean gap 5.21, x = 7).

Chapter 7

Probability and Bayes, with 1,000 people

Percentages mislead. Counts of people do not.

Expert view

Bayes' theorem updates a prior with evidence: P(A | E) = P(E | A) · P(A) / P(E). In practice, skip the formula: imagine 1,000 cases, count how many land in each branch, and divide the ones you care about by all the ones that show the evidence.

The classic error is confusing P(E | A) with P(A | E): "95% of sick people test positive" is not "95% of positives are sick". When the condition is rare, most positives come from the large healthy group.

Chapter 8

Correlation and regression

One number is never the whole picture. Anscombe made the point with four tiny datasets.

Expert view

Pearson's r measures the strength of a straight-line relationship, from −1 to +1. Least-squares regression chooses the line that minimises the sum of squared vertical residuals; R² is the share of the variance in y that the line accounts for (for one predictor, R² = r²).

Both are sensitive to single points. Drag one dot far from the rest and watch the slope and r move. Then press the Anscombe buttons: four datasets with the same mean, variance, r, slope and intercept, and four completely different stories. Always plot before you trust a coefficient.

Chapter 9

Testing a claim: what a p-value really is

Run the same experiment a thousand times and see how often "significant" happens.

Expert view

A p-value is the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the null is true. If there is truly no difference and you test at 0.05, you will still declare a difference in about 1 experiment in 20: that is the Type I error rate, and it is the 5% you chose.

When a real difference exists, the share of experiments with p < 0.05 is the power. It rises with the true difference and with n, and falls with noise. A small, noisy study can have power so low that a "significant" result is more likely an inflated fluke than a discovery.
